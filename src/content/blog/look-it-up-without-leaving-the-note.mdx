You're partway through a paragraph and you hit a word you don't quite know.

It might be a name, a place, a piece of tabernacle furniture, or a term like *ephod* that you've read past a dozen times without ever pinning down. You've been in a good run of thought. So you pick up the phone, open a browser, search, skim three pages, and come back.

The paragraph is still there. The thought isn't.

<mark>The lookup isn't what costs you. Leaving is.</mark>

---

## The tab you never come back from

Anyone who's studied with a laptop open knows how this goes. A search turns into an article, the article into another, and ten minutes later you're reading about something adjacent and interesting that has nothing to do with what you wrote.

You didn't lack discipline. The tool asked you to leave your work in order to get help with it.

I wanted the help to come to the note instead.

## What it does

Select a name, place or term in your note and open its entry in Easton's Bible Dictionary. You get the definition and its cross-references, right beside what you were writing. Close it and the paragraph is where you left it.

Easton's is a classic, freely available Bible dictionary of people, places and terms, first published in 1893 by M.G. Easton. It's good at the ordinary question: *who is this, where is that, what does this word refer to?* It isn't a Greek or Hebrew lexicon, and it won't settle a hard translation question. It's also a nineteenth-century Protestant work, so for anything contested, read it alongside your study Bible or ask your pastor. I'd rather tell you that than have you expect more of it.

It also isn't a research suite, and I don't want it to become one. The goal is enough help to keep going.

## Cross-references keep the trail open

Many Easton's entries point to other passages and to related terms. Those leads are the most useful part, and also the easiest place to wander off.

So the rule I'd suggest: follow one or two while the thought is still warm, then capture what you found before you do anything else. Type the passage as a scripture pill, so the verse stays in your writing. Or highlight the line from the entry that you'll want again.

Then go back to the paragraph. The detour only counts if it ends with something in your note.

## A small method

When you're reading a passage and a word stops you:

1. Don't look it up yet. Finish the sentence you're writing.
2. Select the word and open its entry.
3. Read the definition. Follow one cross-reference if it's clearly relevant.
4. Reread the verse with what you learned, and check that it fits the context. A dictionary gives the general sense; the passage decides which sense is meant.
5. Capture one thing: a pill, a highlight, or a line in your own words.
6. Return to the paragraph and carry on.

Six steps sounds like a lot. In practice it's usually about a minute, and you never lose your place.

## For people newer to the Bible

If you're newer to Scripture, this is probably the feature I'd lean on most. Often nobody stops to explain the vocabulary. A study Bible footnote helps. This is for the small questions the footnote doesn't reach, asked without leaving the note and without feeling you've fallen behind.

You can keep asking those for years, and so can anyone who's been reading for decades. And bring the bigger questions to your pastor or your group; a dictionary entry is a starting point, not the last word.
