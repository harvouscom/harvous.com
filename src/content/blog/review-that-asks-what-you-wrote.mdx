It's October. The class is back, the series has started, and by the second Sunday most of what you covered in the first is gone.

That's not a failing in the room. It's how memory works, and it applies to the person who prepared the lesson too. You spent six hours on the passage, taught it for forty minutes, and a week later you can't say which of your own questions worked.

<mark>The teacher forgets Sunday as reliably as the class does.</mark> We just don't talk about it.

---

## What review usually looks like

When people try to fix this, they reach for one of two things.

The first is a recap: "last week we looked at…" It's useful, and it's one person telling everyone else what mattered. The second is a quiz, which tends to test whether you read the handout, not whether you thought.

I wanted something that sits between them and leaves your own thinking in the middle of it.

## Questions made from your own study

Review exercises in Harvous are built from three things: a note you wrote, a verse you kept, or a chapter you actually read. Never a question someone invented about the text. Nothing is written by a model.

The question types are small and concrete. Finish a verse from memory. Put three of them in the order they come. Pick the verse that belongs to a chapter. Say who appears in it. In the last weeks the fill-in-the-blank ones got word tiles you place, and the put-in-order ones got pieces you arrange, because typing a whole verse into a box is a bad way to find out if you know it.

Every question has a right answer, and Harvous checks it. The result names what was asked, marks the words you got, and shows how the passage actually reads. A question you missed is one you can sit with, not one you've been scored on.

It checks facts. It doesn't judge what a passage means to you or how you chose to say it, because that isn't its job.

## The gap grows as you hold it

How you answer decides when a question comes back. If you needed the reminder, it's back the next day. If you held it a few times in a row, the gap between visits keeps widening. The things you know well stop asking for your attention, and the ones still forming keep showing up.

It's arithmetic, not a model guessing at what you might have forgotten. The same note, the same question, every time, until it stops needing to come back.

There's no streak, no score, no leaderboard. I left those out on purpose. The only thing worth knowing is where that one thing stands.

## Why this is for you, not for the room

I want to be clear about what this isn't. It's not a way to quiz your class, and it doesn't hand your students homework. It works from *your own* notes and chapters, so it's yours.

But that's the part that matters most for a teacher. A class remembers what the teacher is still holding. If you can say in week six what you taught in week one, so can the room, because you can pull the thread back in naturally. <mark>Your own retention is the cheapest teaching tool you have.</mark>
