For most of its life Harvous had a sidebar, and by the end it was doing four things at once.

It showed a little of what you'd been studying lately. It surfaced the notes and passages worth revisiting. It held every organised view of what you'd saved—notes, folders, highlights, scripture. And you searched from it.

Any one of those is reasonable. All four, in a column about 260 pixels wide, permanently taking a quarter of the screen, is where it went wrong.

---

## Four jobs in one column

The trouble with a sidebar is that it charges the same rent to everything inside it.

Your recent study got a few lines. So did what you might want to revisit. So did the list of everything you'd ever written. A column that narrow can only ever offer a glimpse, so each of those things got a glimpse—regardless of whether a glimpse was the right amount.

<mark>It was heavy and cramped at the same time</mark>, which is a strange combination to arrive at. Always there, always taking room, and never actually enough room for the thing you'd opened it for.

## What deserved a whole screen

Once we looked at the four jobs separately, two of them turned out to want opposite things.

Seeing what you've studied is not a glance. It's the thing you'd open the app to do—look at the week, notice what you kept coming back to, find the note from Tuesday you meant to finish. That deserves the whole screen. In a sidebar it got six lines and a scrollbar.

Finding something you saved months ago is the opposite. It deserves no permanent room at all. You need it for about eight seconds, two or three times a session, and the rest of the time it should not be on screen taking up a quarter of your window.

We had been giving both the same narrow strip forever.

## Three views instead of six

So the first half of the answer was to promote one of those jobs. Your study history and what's worth returning to became **Activity**—a view of its own, with the room to actually show a week.

That left Harvous with three core views instead of a column of six: Activity, the Bible reader, and a note. Those are the three things you're ever really doing. You're looking at what you've studied, you're reading Scripture, or you're writing. Everything else is a detour from one of them.

Three is a number you can hold in your head. <mark>Six views down the side was a menu</mark>, and a menu is something you have to read before you can act.

## Search you summon

The second half was the opposite move: take finding things off the screen entirely and make it appear when you ask.

That's the spotlight. Hit it and you get a search field with everything you've saved underneath—notes, folders, threads, highlights, scripture, resources—browsable by kind or searchable by typing. It opens over whatever you were reading, and the note underneath stays exactly where it was.

Which means it does the sidebar's browsing job better than the sidebar did, because it isn't confined to a quarter-width column. It just doesn't sit there when you're not using it.

## What a column really costs

The lesson I'd take from this isn't about sidebars.

It's that permanent screen space is the most expensive thing an app has, and putting something in a fixed panel is a decision to spend it on that thing forever, at the same rate, whether it's earning it or not. Four jobs went into ours because each addition looked small on its own. None of them was wrong. The column was.

<mark>Ask what each job is worth in screen, not whether it fits.</mark> Some of them want everything you have. Most of them want nothing until you ask.

---

If you've used the old sidebar, the thing to notice is what you stopped having to do. You don't pick a view before you look, and you don't give up a quarter of the window to keep the option.
