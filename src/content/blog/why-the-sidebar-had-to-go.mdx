For most of its life Harvous had a sidebar. A rail down the left with six ways to look at your study: notes, folders, highlights, scripture, and so on. It also had a command palette—hit a key, type, get results.

Two features. Both useful. Both, it turned out, asking you a question before you'd done anything.

---

## Two doors, one room

Here is the question they asked: *do you know what you're looking for?*

If you did, the palette was right. Type half a title, hit enter, you're there. If you didn't, the sidebar was right. Switch to highlights, scroll, wait for something to catch.

That sounds like a reasonable division until you watch someone use it. Nobody arrives at their own notes in one of two clean states. You arrive somewhere in between—<mark>you half-remember it</mark>. Something about grace. Written in the spring, maybe. There was a verse in it.

Half-remembering is the normal condition of looking for your own study. And it was the one case the design had nowhere to put.

## What search is actually for

The distinction we'd built on is real, and worth keeping: search is how you retrieve something you can name, and browsing is how you rediscover something you'd forgotten you wrote.

A teacher preparing on Thursday does both in the same minute. You search for the passage you know you covered. You find it. And then you scroll what's near it, because sitting beside it is a note from a class two years ago that you had entirely forgotten and now cannot teach without.

The second thing is not a lesser version of the first. It's often the better one. <mark>The find you didn't go looking for</mark> is most of what makes an old notebook worth keeping.

So the problem was never that we had both. It was that we made you choose the door before you were in the room.

## One panel, seven tabs

What replaced them is a single panel. Tabs across the top for each kind of thing—everything, notes, folders, threads, highlights, scripture, resources—and a search field above them.

Pick a tab and you're browsing. Type and you're searching. Do both and you're searching inside a kind. There is no mode to be in and no shortcut to remember, because the two things are the same panel doing the same job at different levels of certainty.

It opens over whatever you were reading, and the note underneath stays where it was. That matters more than it sounds. Looking something up mid-thought shouldn't cost you the thought.

## What we gave up

The command palette is gone, and I'll admit some affection for it. Palettes are satisfying to build and satisfying to demo. Ours was fast.

It was also a feature you had to be told about. A keyboard shortcut is invisible by definition—there is no way to discover one by looking at the screen. We were teaching it in a tooltip, which is a sign that the thing itself isn't carrying its own explanation.

The sidebar's cost was different. It was always open, always taking a column, and most of the time it was showing you a list you weren't reading. Screen you have to give up permanently for a tool you need occasionally is a bad trade, and it's a worse one on a laptop.

## The rule underneath

<mark>A tool shouldn't ask you to classify your own question</mark> before it will help you answer it.

That's the whole principle, and it applies well beyond a sidebar. Every time software makes you pick a mode first, it's moved a decision from the machine to you—and it's the kind of decision you're least equipped to make at the start, when you know the least about what you're looking for.

We had built two good tools and made you sort your own thinking into one of them at the door. The fix wasn't a better sidebar. It was noticing that the sorting was ours to do, not yours.

---

The Library is in Harvous now. If you've used the old rail, the thing to try is the one that used to be awkward: don't decide what you're looking for. Just open it and start typing what you half-remember.
