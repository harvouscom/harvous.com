The fall is when the blank note is at its worst.

Classes start back up. Small groups reconvene after a summer off. Someone asks you to take the new series, and you open a fresh page on a Tuesday night and look at it.

Nothing is wrong with that page. It's just the hardest one to write on. <mark>Almost every good study starts from a shape someone else already found.</mark>

---

## You don't have to invent the method

Teachers and preachers have been handing each other shapes for a long time. Observe, then interpret, then apply. Scripture, observation, application, prayer. A hook, the text, a look at it, then a call to change. None of these are mine, and none of them are secrets.

What's missing is usually somewhere to put them where you'll actually use them. A shape in a PDF is a shape you meant to open.

That's the job of Discover. It's a small catalog of things you can add to your own Harvous with one tap, and the things that matter for a new term are the templates:

- **Study methods** for a class or a personal pass through a book: [Inductive Study](/discover/inductive-study/), SOAP, TEXT, Topical Study, Comparative Study.
- **Sermon and lesson outlines**: HBLT, Me, We, God, You, We, the Three-Act Structure, the Sticky Sermon, the classic three-point outline, and Lane Sebring's four-step method.

You pick one, add it, and it's a template in your notes like any other. It comes with the headings already there, so the first thing you do is write, not set up.

## Make it yours on the first Tuesday

The point isn't to follow a template to the letter. A template that fits every class doesn't exist, and a good teacher breaks the shape on purpose.

The point is that <mark>it's easier to cut a heading you don't need than to remember one you forgot</mark>. Add HBLT, notice you have no real hook this week, and write one anyway. Or delete the Application section because this lesson is about orientation, and leave a note to yourself about why.

Then, because it's a Harvous note, the passage stays linked to what you wrote about it. Next week, when someone asks what you said about verse 12, it's not in a pile of loose pages.

## For the person leading the class

If you're the one preparing, I'd start with two things.

Add a method you'd use for your own reading, so your prep and your class share a shape. And read [the lesson prep stack](/blog/the-lesson-prep-stack): five parts, passage, aim, questions, trail, return, and there's a [template for it in Discover](/discover/guide-the-lesson-prep-stack/) you can add to your own notes.

If you're going to teach through a book this term, [prep a lesson that leaves a trail](/blog/prep-a-lesson-that-leaves-a-trail) is the companion piece.

## What Discover isn't

It isn't a curriculum, and it isn't a marketplace. Most of what's in it is a shape or a doorway into someone else's good work. Alongside the templates are links out to places I trust, like BibleProject's overview videos or Working Preacher's weekly commentary, and those open on the publisher's own site.

I'd rather point you at the best thing than rebuild it badly.

---

If your class or group starts up this month, <mark>pick one shape and use it for the first four weeks</mark>. Don't optimize it. Run it long enough that you can tell what to change.
