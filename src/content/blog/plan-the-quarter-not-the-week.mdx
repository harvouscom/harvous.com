Ask most churches for their teaching plan and you get a spreadsheet.

Column A is dates. Column B is whoever said yes. Somewhere around week five the passages stop and the cells go blank, because that is as far as anyone has thought.

Nobody is being lazy. A quarter gets planned the way a week gets planned, thirteen times in a row, and each pass only has to survive until Sunday. It works. It just never adds up to anything.

<mark>A list of Sundays is not a plan.</mark> A plan has a shape before week one.

---

## A list of Sundays isn't a plan

Here is the tell. Ask a teacher in week seven what week ten is about.

If the honest answer is "I'll look at it in a couple weeks," the room is being taught one hour at a time. That is survivable, and a lot of faithful teaching happens that way. But it costs something you only notice later:

- Week nine can't build on week three, because nobody remembers deciding what week three was for
- A substitute inherits a date and a passage and none of the reasoning
- The quarter ends and no one can say what it was *about*, only what it covered

Coverage is not an arc. A room can sit through eleven weeks of Romans and leave with eleven disconnected hours.

---

## What a plan has to hold

This part is tool-agnostic. Whether you use a whiteboard, a shared doc, or software, every entry in a teaching plan needs five things:

**The date.** Not "week 4" — the actual Sunday. Weeks slip. Dates argue back.

**The passage.** Written out and checked. A mistyped reference in a plan becomes a mistyped reference in forty people's notes.

**The aim, in one sentence.** What you want this hour to do. If it takes a paragraph, it isn't an aim yet.

**The series it belongs to.** Not as decoration. As the thing that makes week nine make sense.

**Who is teaching it.** Named early, so a gap is visible in August instead of on a Saturday night.

That's it. Five fields, thirteen times. It fits on one page and it is more plan than most churches have.

## A series is a thing, not a label

This is the part that quietly breaks.

In most plans a series is a word you retype at the top of each week. "Romans." "Romans." "Roman." Now you have two series and one of them has three weeks in it.

Rename it in week five and weeks one through four keep the old name, because they were never actually connected — they just happened to agree with each other for a while.

A series should be one thing you can open. Name it once and every week carries it. Change the name and every week changes with you. That sounds like a small mechanical detail. It is the difference between <mark>a run you can see and thirteen weeks that resemble each other</mark>.

It also changes how you plan. When a series knows it runs eight weeks, the eight weeks exist from the day you name it — five of them empty, waiting. You are filling in a shape rather than adding to a pile.

---

## Room for the undated idea

Every teacher has a list somewhere: passages worth a series, a question the congregation keeps circling, the book you've wanted to do for two years.

Those ideas usually die in a notes app because a plan has no room for anything without a date on it. So the good idea waits for a slot that never gets offered, and week ten gets filled with whatever was easy in August.

Give undated ideas a place to sit *next to* the plan. Not on the calendar — beside it. Then planning the back half of a quarter starts from a shelf of things you actually wanted to teach, instead of a blank cell and a deadline.

---

## See what's still unwritten

A plan should be honest about its own holes.

If week eleven has a date and nothing else, it should *look* like that everywhere it shows up — on the board, on the calendar, in the list. The moment an empty week looks the same as a finished one, the plan stops telling you the truth and you find out in November.

This is the whole argument for planning a quarter at once. Not so everything gets written in August. So that in August you can see <mark>which weeks are still owed</mark>, while there's time to do something about it.

Try one question in your next staff or teachers' meeting: *which weeks between now and the end of the quarter have a date and nothing else?* If nobody can answer without opening four files, the plan isn't a plan yet.
