Most software that tracks a habit has the same instinct about the days you missed: hide them.

Not maliciously. A streak counter is trying to be encouraging, and a run of grey squares isn't encouraging. So the interface quietly rewrites your month into the parts that went well, and the gaps become an absence—a number that reset, a chain that broke.

We had to decide what Harvous would do with the days nobody studied. The answer we landed on is that <mark>the empty days stay</mark>. They get a page in the record like any other day, and the page says nothing happened.

---

## What a streak is really for

Worth being honest about who streaks serve. A streak is a retention mechanic, in the business sense of retention—it exists to bring you back tomorrow. It works by creating something you can lose.

That's a real force and it does change behaviour. But look at what it measures. A streak knows whether you opened the app. It does not know whether you understood anything, and it cannot tell the difference between a morning you sat with a passage for forty minutes and a morning you tapped through a verse to keep the number alive.

For a habit where showing up *is* the whole thing, that's a fair trade. Bible study isn't that habit. Nobody has ever been helped by a chapter they skimmed to protect a counter.

## Study is not daily by nature

The deeper problem is that the streak assumes a shape your study doesn't have.

Ask anyone who teaches. Their year has terms in it. There's a stretch in the autumn where they're deep in a book every week because a class depends on it, and there's the fortnight after Christmas where they read nothing and needed to. A term ends and the intensity drops on purpose, not by failure.

A streak reads that as eleven days lost. The teacher would read it as a term finishing. <mark>One of those is a description of what happened</mark> and the other is a scold.

## The shape of a real year

Here's what a record can show you that a counter can't.

Look back over a few months in Harvous and you can see when you were in something. Not how many days in a row—*where the weight was*. A dense fortnight in Romans in March. A quieter April. A cluster in June where the same three chapters keep coming back because you were building something around them.

That shape is genuinely useful. It tells you what you actually gave your attention to, as opposed to what you meant to. And you can only see it if the light stretches are still on the page next to the heavy ones. Take out the empty days and every month looks equally busy, which is another way of saying no month tells you anything.

## Why the empty days stay

So a day with nothing in it still gets a sheet in Harvous, and the sheet says so plainly.

It's a small thing to build and it was an easy call once we'd said the alternative out loud. A record that silently drops the days you didn't study is a record that rewrites your month into an unbroken run you never had. It would be a more flattering month. It would also be fiction, and you'd have no reason to trust the rest of it.

## What the gaps tell you

The practical payoff is that gaps become information rather than guilt.

A fortnight of nothing in the middle of a series is worth noticing—not because you failed, but because something crowded it out and you may want to know what. Three weeks off after a class ends is not a lapse, it's a rhythm, and seeing it repeat across a year is how you learn to plan the next one.

<mark>Guilt is not a study aid.</mark> It reliably produces the feeling of having a problem and just as reliably fails to produce a solution. Information is better, and the gaps are information.

---

None of this means the record does the work. It doesn't. It just tells you the truth about a month when you go looking, which is the least a record owes you—and more than a number that only knows whether you turned up.
