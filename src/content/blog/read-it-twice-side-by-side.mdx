Most of us read the same translation for years, and it's fine. It's the one that sounds like Scripture to us.

The trouble is that it sounds the same every time. A word that carries the whole verse reads smoothly past you, because you've heard it that way a hundred times, and you never notice it was a choice.

<mark>One translation shows you how a verse was rendered. Two show you where the rendering was a choice.</mark>

---

## Why bother with a second version

Translators make decisions. Some stay close to the word order and wording of the original, and others aim for natural English, so the same verse can read differently without anyone disagreeing about what it means. Sometimes the Hebrew or Greek has two good English options and they pick one. And occasionally the oldest manuscripts differ, and translators weigh them differently. Those places are few and well marked, and they rarely touch anything central to what the church teaches.

You don't need to know any of that to benefit. You only need to notice where two versions differ. A place where the ESV and the NET use different words is worth a second look. Sometimes it's only style. Sometimes the Greek or Hebrew can honestly carry both, and that's where a good question usually lives.

It's especially useful when you teach. A class asks "why does my Bible say it differently?" less often than you'd think, but when they do, you want to have already looked.

## What it looks like in Harvous

Open a chapter in the reader. Beside the translation chip there's a `+`. Tap it and a second version opens next to the first. You're on the same chapter at the same spot, with your highlights still showing. The page just gets wider to hold another translation. The `x` closes it again.

On a phone, the two versions take turns and you swipe between them. Two columns of Scripture at that width isn't readable, and I'd rather not pretend it is.

There are eleven English versions to pair: KJV, NKJV, ESV, NIV, NLT, NET, BSB, NASB 1995, CSB, AMP and MSG. Pairing versions with different approaches teaches the most, like the ESV beside the NLT, because two near-identical ones show you little. The Amplified Bible and The Message are looser, so they're good for hearing a verse fresh, not for building a point on.

## Verse 7 beside verse 7

This is the part that's easy to get wrong, and the part I cared about most.

Translations don't agree on how many verses a chapter has. A few verses are printed in some versions and moved to a footnote in others, because the earliest manuscripts don't all agree. Matthew 17:21 is a familiar example.

If you line up two chapters by walking down both lists together, every row after the first disagreement is wrong, quietly, for the rest of the chapter. You'd be comparing verse 38 against verse 37 and never know.

So Harvous matches rows on the verse number itself. Verse 7 sits beside verse 7 all the way down. When one version doesn't print a verse, the cell says which version is missing it, instead of sliding the next verse up to hide the gap.

## Mark either side

Highlighting isn't turned off on the second column. If the phrase you want is clearer in the NET than in the ESV, highlight it there. It joins the same highlight layer as everything else, and it's waiting for you when you open that chapter again on its own.

The second translation is part of the link, too. Turn the page and the comparison holds. Send the link to someone, and they see what you were reading.

## What to do with the difference

Spotting a difference isn't the work. The work is deciding what it means, and I'd keep that part small:

1. Read the chapter in your usual translation first.
2. Add a second one and go down it once, looking only for places the wording changes.
3. Highlight the two or three that matter.
4. In a note, write one sentence: what is different, and what question does it raise?
5. Check a study Bible's footnote or a commentary. The notes in the ESV and the NET often explain the choice.

Then stop. You aren't writing a paper. You've found a good question and looked for an answer, and that's enough for one sitting.
